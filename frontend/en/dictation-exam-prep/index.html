Music dictation exam prep

In a dictation exam you listen to music and write it down as notation. Many conservatories and music schools test it alongside music theory and sight-singing, and it improves steadily with daily practice. This page covers the basics and how to practice.

Try it: hearing notes from a reference

The foundation of dictation is hearing how far a note is from a reference note. Try it in C major.

The main types of dictation

Melodic dictation

Write down a single melody: pitches, rhythm (note values, rests, ties) and accidentals. In memory dictation you listen first and write afterwards.

Two-part dictation

Write an upper and a lower line played together on a grand staff. Don't let the top voice distract you from the bass.

Harmonic (four-part) dictation

Write a progression for soprano, alto, tenor and bass. Start with the outer voices, then use the chord functions (tonic, subdominant, dominant) to fill in the inner voices.

An example: Tokyo University of the Arts

The 2027 entrance exam guide of Tokyo University of the Arts (Faculty of Music) lists a written dictation test of about 30 minutes for composition, voice, instrumental and musicology applicants: one melody (memory dictation), one two-part piece and one four-part harmony. Formats change from year to year and differ between schools, so always check the official admissions information.

Daily practice tips

  1. Start with single notes and intervals. Knowing the distance between notes makes melodies much easier.
  2. Hear the key first. Play the tonic chord and feel "do" before you listen.
  3. Learn the sound of chords. Telling major, minor and dominant seventh chords apart helps with harmonic dictation.
  4. Write the rhythm too. Count the beats while you listen.
  5. A little every day. 10–15 minutes daily beats one long session a week.
